Florida combines U.S. federal employment obligations with a limited state payroll-tax layer. There is no Florida individual income tax. Employers must still administer Florida reemployment tax, minimum-wage changes, workers’ compensation coverage, new-hire reporting, E-Verify obligations where applicable, wage-and-hour rules, and federal payroll requirements.

Scope note. This page is an operational compliance checklist, not legal or tax advice. Verify the payroll-year rate, exact work location, employer size, industry classification, federal coverage, workers’ compensation threshold, and local or contractual requirements before onboarding or payroll setup.

At-a-glance

2026 minimum wage

USD 14.00 per hour applies through September 29, 2026. The Florida minimum wage increases to USD 15.00 per hour on September 30, 2026. The cash wage for tipped employees is subject to the state’s permitted tip credit.

State income tax

No Florida personal income tax. Florida employers do not withhold state individual income tax from wages; federal withholding remains required.

2026 reemployment tax

2.70% is the initial rate for a new liable employer and generally remains in place until the employer has reported for the required experience period.

Taxable wage base

Florida reemployment tax is employer-paid and applies to the first USD 7,000 in annual wages paid to each employee.

Employment standards

Overtime and records

Florida generally relies on federal FLSA overtime rules. Covered nonexempt employees generally receive 1.5 times their regular rate for hours worked over 40 in a workweek. Maintain federal time, wage, deduction, and payroll records, and validate all exempt classifications.

Pay frequency and final pay

Florida does not set a general statewide pay-frequency rule or a general statutory deadline for final wages. Establish a consistent written pay schedule and issue final wages under the written policy, contract, and regular payday practice. Check collective bargaining, commission, and vacation terms before separation.

Breaks and paid leave

Florida does not generally require meal or rest breaks for adult employees or statewide paid sick leave or paid family leave. Federal rules can require payment for short breaks, and employer policies or local rules must be followed. Minors are subject to separate break and child-labor protections.

Workers’ compensation

Coverage turns on industry and headcount. Construction employers generally need coverage with one or more employees; non-construction employers generally need it with four or more employees; agricultural employers generally need it with six regular employees or 12 qualifying seasonal workers.

Onboarding controls

  • Confirm the employee’s Florida work address, remote-work arrangement, job duties, compensation plan, and whether the engagement is in construction, agriculture, public sector, or another regulated industry.
  • Complete federal Form I-9 within the federal deadline. Under Florida law, private employers with 25 or more employees, public agencies, and covered employee leasing companies must use E-Verify for new hires within three business days after the employee begins work for pay.
  • Collect federal Form W-4 and configure federal income-tax withholding, FICA, Medicare, FUTA, and annual Form W-2; do not configure Florida state income-tax withholding.
  • Register for Florida reemployment tax when liable, file Form RT-6 quarterly even when required without reportable wages, and pay the employer tax by the applicable quarterly deadline.
  • Report new hires and rehires to the Florida New Hire Reporting Center within 20 days.
  • Obtain Florida-compliant workers’ compensation coverage when the applicable industry/headcount threshold is met; confirm Florida is listed on the policy for out-of-state employers operating in Florida.
  • Post the required Florida reemployment assistance notice, federal labor posters, the applicable Florida minimum-wage poster, and industry-specific workplace notices.
  • Document exempt/nonexempt status, wage basis, scheduled paydays, worksite, reimbursement arrangements, required policy acknowledgments, and any commission, bonus, PTO, or separation-pay terms.

Classification and separation

Employee versus contractor

Worker status is fact-dependent and can vary across federal tax, FLSA, reemployment tax, and workers’ compensation regimes. The Florida Department of Revenue treats an employee for reemployment-tax purposes as an individual in a permanent position who performs services under the employer’s control or direction. Escalate every contractor engagement for state and federal review.

Termination controls

Florida is generally an at-will employment jurisdiction absent a contract, policy commitment, collective bargaining agreement, or statutory protection. Review retaliation, discrimination, protected leave, wage claims, notice provisions, restrictive covenants, commission plans, and benefits obligations before any separation. Pay earned wages according to the regular payday and written agreement where no earlier requirement applies.

Local-law review

Florida state law is a baseline rather than a full worksite analysis. Verify city and county rules, client-site requirements, federal-contract requirements, and industry wage orders before making an offer or activating payroll. Apply an enforceable higher wage, leave, notice, or benefit standard when it governs the employment relationship.
